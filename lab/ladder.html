Every clock has a comma.

Musicians think the comma is their private misery. It isn't. Any time two cycles refuse to divide into each other, one of them leaves a little remainder on the floor, and somebody has to decide what to do about it. Calendar makers have been sweeping up after the sun and the moon since before anybody wrote music down.

Below, pick a pair of clocks. Each dot is one more tick of the first clock, laid around the face of the second. If the two ever lined up exactly, the dots would stack into a few straight spokes and stop making anything new. They never do.

01

The spiral never closes.

Pick two clocks

1,000

Watch the spokes. With few dots the eye finds a handful of arms. Add more and those arms twist, smear, and give way to a larger count, then a larger one still. Each count is a near miss: that many steps of the first clock come almost back to the start of the second. Almost is the whole story. Every count fails eventually, and a new one takes over.

02

The ladder of near misses.

The near misses aren't random. They're the rungs of a continued fraction, the oldest trick there is for approximating a number that won't come out even. Each rung is the best you can do with cycles that short, and each leaves a remainder, which is that rung's comma. Pick one to put its spokes on the spiral.

Rungs

For the musician, rung 12 is the piano: twelve fifths against seven octaves, off by 23.46 cents. Rung 53 is the one the Chinese theorist Jing Fang found around 40 BC and Nicholas Mercator wrote up in the 1600s: fifty-three fifths against thirty-one octaves, off by 3.6 cents. For the calendar maker, rung 4 is Julius Caesar's leap year, and the 19-year rung of the moon is the Metonic cycle that still sets the date of Easter. Same arithmetic, different arguments.

03

How far down can you read?

The ladder goes down forever. Nobody can use all of it, because nobody can tell apart differences smaller than their own noise. Set how fine your instrument is and see which rung is the first one good enough to fool it.

Your resolution

That is what the note on the tuning channel means when it says the spiral is infinite and your resolution isn't, and capacity is the quotient. The dots never run out. What runs out is your ability to tell one from the next. It's a good thing to remember the next time somebody sells you infinite anything.

04

The sunflower picked the worst number on purpose.

Press the golden ratio up top and look at what grows. That's a sunflower head, near enough. A sunflower lays down each new seed a fixed turn past the last one, about 137.5 degrees, and that turn is built from the golden ratio, the number with the slowest ladder of all. Its continued fraction is nothing but ones, so every rung is as poor an approximation as a rung can be, and the seeds never settle into a few spokes that would waste the space between them. The arms you do see come in Fibonacci counts, 21 and 34 and 55, because those are the golden ratio's rungs.

A piano wants the opposite. It wants a rung good enough to close the circle, and it settles for twelve. The sunflower wants a circle that never closes, so it can keep packing seeds forever without any two lining up. Plants worked out the comma's other use a few hundred million years before Pythagoras, and nobody had to hold a meeting about it.

How it's drawn. Dot n sits at angle 2π·n·x and radius proportional to √n (Vogel's model of a sunflower head), where x is the ratio of the two clocks; only its fractional part matters for the angle. The rungs are the convergents p/q of x's continued fraction, computed in double precision, so the deepest rungs of very long expansions stop being trustworthy after about twelve terms; the page stops there. A rung's miss is |q·x − p| in the second clock's units, converted to cents, minutes, days, or years where that means something, and to degrees of the circle otherwise. The resolution panel asks for the first rung whose error per step, the miss divided by q, falls below your resolution. Astronomical values: tropical year 365.24219 days, synodic month 29.530589 days, Jupiter's period 11.862 years, Saturn's 29.457 years. The Gregorian calendar's 97 leap days in 400 years is not a rung of the year's continued fraction; it is a committee's compromise that sits between rungs and is easy to count on fingers, which is a perfectly good reason.
