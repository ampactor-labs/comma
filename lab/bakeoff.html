Which tuning thinks best?

The main page builds its machine out of the piano's errors. But the piano is only one way of being wrong. For five hundred years tuners fought over how to spread the comma around, and every one of their answers leaves a different fan of mistakes. So hold a contest. Build the bank out of each tuning's errors and see which one remembers the most.

Nobody in those arguments was thinking about memory. They were thinking about whether the organ in church sounded like a sack of cats in E-flat. The results don't care what they were thinking.

01

The contestants.

Each card is one tuning's bank: eleven clocks, one per interval from the main page, each turning at the rate that tuning misses its interval by, slowed down six times so you can follow them. A clock that stands still is an interval the tuning gets exactly right. Exactly right means it has nothing to spend.

Eleven clocks per tuning

Each tuning gets the same experiment the main page runs: the same input, the same coupling of 0.12, the same readout, scored on data it never saw. Tap a card for its numbers.

The selected tuning

IntervalMisses byBeat at A3

Look at who wins. Equal temperament, the tuning that won the actual war, lands in the middle of the pack. Two of the well-temperaments of the 1700s, Kirnberger's and Vallotti's, give the bank more to work with than the modern piano does. They spread their errors unevenly, and none of their clocks freeze. Meantone, which bought pure thirds by hammering its fifths flat, does worse. And just intonation, the purist's dream, freezes four of its eleven clocks dead and pays for every one of them.

02

The better the tuning, the dumber the machine.

Now forget history and walk the ladder of equal divisions of the octave, from five notes up to sixty. More notes means every interval can be matched more closely. That's the dream of every microtonal reformer since Mercator. Here's what it does to the bank.

Equal divisions, five to sixty

capacity, delays 0 to 300 reach, seconds average error, cents
Fifty-six experiments. They land one at a time.

As the errors shrink, capacity sinks. The curve is jagged, because each division happens to land near some intervals and far from others, but the trend is plain: the best banks come from somewhere between nine and twenty notes, and past about twenty-five it's downhill. Up at fifty-three, Mercator's beautiful near-perfect tuning, the bank holds a little over half what the piano's does. Reach goes the other way, because slow clocks hold the past longer, but they hold less of it, and they hold it as near-copies of each other. You can have a tuning so accurate that the machine built from its errors barely notices anything happened. Perfection is a fine thing to aim at. It is a poor place to live.

03

Can anybody beat the piano?

Throw away tuning altogether. Keep the band the piano lives in, 0.37 to 8.63 Hz, and let a search pick any eleven rates it likes inside it. The search here is the simplest one that works, the kind a desert rat could run on a pocket calculator: change the rates a little at random, keep the change if the bank holds more, repeat.

A blind search over eleven rates

It measures the piano, an even spacing, and an even spacing on a log scale first, then starts from the piano and wanders.

The search does beat the piano, but not by much, and the plainest fan you could draw with a ruler, eleven rates evenly spaced, does nearly as well as the search does. The piano's fan is lopsided, with four clocks crowded below 0.7 Hz, and crowding is redundancy. Still, an instrument designed by monks, merchants, and committees for a completely different purpose gets most of the way to the best this machine allows. Accidents are like that sometimes.

04

A bigger bank.

The main page stops at eleven intervals, the harmonics up to 23. Keep going up the harmonic series and every new harmonic brings a new error, a new clock, and three more numbers for the readout. Does the bank keep up with its own ceiling?

Eleven to forty-seven clocks

Each bank uses the odd harmonics 3, 5, 7, and on up, folded into one octave above A3 and measured against the piano.

More clocks hold more, but each new one is worth a little less, because the new clocks have to squeeze into the same narrow band of speeds as the old ones. The ceiling grows by three with every clock. Capacity grows slower. Crowd enough tuning forks into a small room and they start finishing each other's sentences.

Method. Every bar on this page is the main page's experiment, run by lab/kit.js, which carries the same equations and constants and reproduces the main page's numbers exactly when given the piano's fan (node tools/verify.mjs checks this, and checks the orderings this page's prose claims). Coupling K = 0.12, seeded random input, 3,500 steps to fit and 3,500 to score. Capacity here is summed over delays 0 to 300, the main page's "longer window", because the 0-to-30 window hides most of what slow banks remember; the cards also report the 0-to-30 figure. Reach is the last delay probed in tens from 40 to 260 steps whose held-out score is at least 0.01. A tuning's detuning for each interval is the frequency gap at A3 = 220 Hz between the just interval and the nearest note that tuning has, with the temperaments rooted on A. Historical temperaments are defined by how much each fifth around the circle is narrowed: Werckmeister III and Vallotti in fractions of the Pythagorean comma, quarter-comma meantone and Kirnberger III in fractions of the syntonic comma and the schisma, with the leftover in meantone and Pythagorean going to the wolf between G♯ and E♭. The search is a (1+1) evolution strategy over log-rates with a step that grows on success and shrinks on failure, signs kept from the piano, capped at 160 experiments; another run with another seed would find a slightly different fan. The bank never sees anything but random input here, so "thinks best" means "holds the most of a random signal", one narrow kind of thinking.
