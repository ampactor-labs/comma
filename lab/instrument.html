Back country · 07 · The bank with a hand on it

Play the Bank

Up the trail, on the main page, the bank only ever hears dice: a seeded random signal, the same one every time, fed in by a machine that never gets bored. Down here you are the weather. Put a hand on it and it answers.

Eleven damped oscillators, each one turning at the speed of a piano interval's mistake, from the fifth's lazy 0.37 Hz to the 11th harmonic's 8.63. Same equations as the main page, same constants, stepped 50 times a second in your browser. Drag the pad, hit the keys, holler at it if you like.

01

Put a hand on it.

Each comet is one oscillator, a point in the complex plane around a shared origin. Distance out is how hard it rings. Angle is where it is in its turn. Push the input and all eleven take the same shove, then each one spins it off at its own wrong speed, the way one thunderstorm comes down eleven side canyons and reaches the river at eleven different hours.

The hot amber ones are the intervals the piano gets most wrong, and they spin fastest. The cool cyan stragglers are the near misses, nearly tame.

    02

    It remembers you, a little.

    Nobody trains the bank. A readout learns to read it: 34 weights fit by least squares on the main page's random signal. This page fits the same kind of readout when it loads, one for each delay, then turns them loose on you. The amber line is the bank's guess at what you did k steps ago. When it lands on the dashed line, that is the bank remembering you.

    Crank the coupling and the oscillators start agreeing with each other, like a county commission. Shrink the errors toward nothing and the eleven comets fold into one. Watch what either does to the scores.

    03

    A drift clock.

    The main page has a second machine, a register that holds ten letters as phase offsets and loses them as the coupling argues the oscillators back toward agreement. Write the time into it and walk away. Before it starts, this page runs the same register ahead in fast-forward and writes down when each letter will die. After that, counting the dead tells you roughly how long it has been, the way a geologist reads a cutbank if she knows how fast the river lays down mud.

    It is a lousy clock. It tells time by forgetting, which is how most clocks in the desert work.

    What this is. The live bank is Kit.bench(OMEGA).makeLive(K), the same equations and constants as the main page (damping 0.6 per second, input gain 0.30, step 0.02 s, mean-field coupling K), with the eleven detunings of 12-tone equal temperament above A3 = 220 Hz. It is stepped at exactly 50 steps per second of wall-clock time; if the tab stalls it skips ahead rather than racing to catch up, and it stops when the tab is hidden. Your input is clamped to ±0.8. Keys and MIDI notes are short kicks that decay with a 0.12 s time constant; the microphone maps loudness from −60 to −10 dBFS onto 0 to 0.8; the noise button feeds uniform random values in ±0.8 each step, the same distribution the experiment uses.

    The readouts are ridge fits on the main page's seeded random input (500 steps to settle, 3,500 to fit, 3,500 to score), one per delay k ∈ {0, 10, 25, 50, 100}, at the current coupling and error size. The scores shown are held-out squared correlations on that random input. They are then applied, unchanged, to whatever you feed in. Your gestures are nothing like white noise: slow drags and held values push the oscillators into their nonlinear range, where the fit was never trained, so the reconstruction can be better or worse than the score says. The "on your input" line measures that directly, as the squared correlation between the amber line and your actual input k steps back over the last eight seconds. Even at k = 0 the readout is far from perfect, because the fresh input arrives mixed into eleven states that already hold the past.

    The drift clock is the main page's phase register (Kit.registerStep, a Kuramoto model at the same eleven rates) with its own 32-symbol alphabet so it can spell digits. The forecast and the live register run the identical arithmetic with the identical fixed step, so the live letters die at exactly the forecast steps. The live register is advanced by the browser's own clock, so it can never be more accurate than the clock that drives it. What it shows is that a known rate of forgetting is a clock, and a coarse one: the bracket is as wide as the gap between two deaths.
