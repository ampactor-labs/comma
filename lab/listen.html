The Listening Test

A note in this repo claims a piano's tuning can hide about 290 bits before anyone in the hall notices. The arithmetic is fine. The two numbers holding it up were guessed at a desk. This page puts your ears on the witness stand instead.

You hear the same thing twice and say which one somebody tampered with. That's the whole game. It takes ten minutes or so and wants a quiet room, headphones if you have them, and no dog barking at the mail truck. Nothing you do here leaves this browser unless you send it yourself.

01

Set up camp

First the gear. Tell the page how you're listening, then play the reference chord and set the level where you'd leave it for an evening by the fire: clearly there, nowhere near loud. Leave it there for both tests.

Output and level

Phone speaker plays everything two octaves above concert pitch, because a small speaker can't reproduce much below 500 Hz. Every beat runs four times as fast up there, which probably makes both tests easier. The register goes into your results.

70%

Then two practice trials, one of each test, with the tampering cranked so far that a raven on a fence post would notice. Miss these and check the cable before you blame your ears.

02

A doctored temperament

Equal temperament is a treaty signed in the eighteenth century and enforced ever since by men carrying tuning hammers. Here is a five-chord walk, C, A minor, F, G and back to C, played twice. One of the two runs on the treaty. In the other, six of the twelve pitch classes are pushed sharp by Δ cents and the other six flat by the same Δ, chosen fresh each trial. Which one was doctored?

Get two right in a row and Δ shrinks. Miss one and it grows. The staircase walks down the canyon until it's camped at the size you catch about 71 percent of the time, and that's your number.

03

Shaded unisons

The tenor and treble notes on a piano each have three strings. A tuner can leave them a hair apart and the note blooms a little, the way a choir is warmer than one man singing in a canyon. The note bets you'll hear a spread of ±1.5 cents as warmth and not as error. Below, one A has its three strings dead level. The other has one string Δ cents flat, one in the middle, one Δ sharp. Which one was shaded?

Listen to the tail, not the strike. Whatever there is to hear lives in the slow swell as the note dies.

04

What your ears say the piano can hold

A forced choice between two runs, back to back, with replays and your full attention, is a harder exam than any concert. Nobody in the fourth row is told that one of two things is wrong and asked to point at it. So the thresholds you get here are smaller than what an audience would catch, and the channel computed from them is a floor. If anything the piano holds more.

05

Measure a real piano

If there's a real piano within reach, the kind in a church basement or a bar with sawdust on the floor, strike an interval and let it ring. Where a partial of the low note nearly lands on a partial of the high one, the two beat. For a fifth that's the low note's third partial against the high note's second. Count the beats and you have the tuning error, give or take what the strings get up to on their own.

The microphone feeds a loudness envelope a hundred times a second. The page looks for a slow, steady wobble in it and turns that wobble's rate into cents.

What was measured, and how. Test A plays C–Am–F–G–C voiced C3 E4 G4 C5, A2 E4 A4 C5, F2 F4 A4 C5, G2 D4 G4 B4, C3 E4 G4 C5, chords 0.8 s apart with the last left to ring 1.9 s, through Kit.sound.pluck: six partials, each slightly sharp (the factor 1 + 0.0004h², a token stiff-string stretch), with an exponential decay. The doctored run moves a random six of the twelve pitch classes up by Δ and the other six down by Δ. Seven pitch classes appear in the progression, so at least one always moves against the rest and the doctoring is never a plain transposition. Every doctored pitch class sits at the edge of a ±Δ window, so some intervals are off by as much as 2Δ. Test B is A4 (440 Hz, or 1760 Hz on the phone setting) as three plucks of 3.4 s struck together in phase, at 0, 0, 0 or −Δ, 0, +Δ cents.

Both tests are two-interval forced choice with the doctored run's position random, and a 2-down-1-up staircase (Levitt, 1971) that converges near 70.7% correct. Δ starts at 12 cents (A) or 4 cents (B), moves by a factor of 1.5, then 1.2 from the second reversal on, and is capped at 40 or 16 cents. A run stops at 8 reversals or 40 trials. The threshold is the geometric mean of the Δ at the last six reversals. A run that ends with fewer than two reversals reports the geometric mean of its last six levels and is flagged as unconverged.

A forced choice measures discrimination: an alert listener, a reference next to the target, as many replays as wanted. An audience has none of that, so these thresholds sit below the audience detection window the note asks about, and the recomputed channel is conservative. The results also depend on the speaker or headphones, the register, the room, the level, practice and fatigue, and on one progression in one key. A real piano's partials are louder, longer and more inharmonic than these. One listener is one listener.

The channel uses the note's own formulas with your thresholds rounded to 0.01 cent. Temperament: levels = ⌊2A/2⌋ + 1 at the note's 2-cent strobe resolution, bits = 12·log2(levels). Per-key stretch: the test doesn't measure it, so the page takes the note's 88·log23 ≈ 139.5 bits and scales it linearly by A/5, capped at 1, because a stretch wiggle should be no bolder than the temperament window it hides inside. A good ear can shrink this term but never grow it. Unisons: levels = ⌊2B/1⌋ + 1 at 1-cent resolution, two strings by 60 notes, halved for stability. The note's inputs (5 and 1.5 cents) give 31.0 + 139.5 + 120.0 = 290.5 bits.

The beat meter takes a 40 ms Hann-weighted RMS every 10 ms. It starts 0.25 s after the loudest point in the window (the strike), divides the envelope by a quadratic fit to its logarithm to take out the decay, applies a Hann window, and evaluates a direct Fourier transform from max(0.2 Hz, 1.5/T) to 20 Hz on a grid no coarser than min(0.02, 1/4T) Hz, then refines the tallest peak. T is the analyzed length. Confidence is the share of the band's power within ±2/T of the peak, times min(1, cycles/3), times min(1, depth/0.2%) where depth is the beat's modulation depth, and cut to 30% for a peak pinned to the bottom of the band. The cents figure assumes harmonic partials, c = 1200·log2(1 + b/(p·flow)), and treats the lower note as sitting at equal-tempered pitch with A4 = 440 Hz. Beats can't tell sharp from flat. Real strings are stiff and their partials run sharp, which moves coincident partials and so the beat rate, more at the ends of the keyboard. Browsers may process the microphone signal even when asked not to. The synthetic test uses the same estimator on a dyad whose beat is known exactly.
