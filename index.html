Tuning · Memory · Machines

The piano is
lying to you.

Every note on it is slightly wrong, on purpose, and has been for three hundred years. This page is about that lie: how to hear it, why we agreed to it, and what you can build out of the leftover.

Press a button. You'll need sound. Anything that holds a note keeps holding it until you press the same button again, and the control in the corner stops everything at once.

Two fifths

A fifth is C up to G. There are two ways to tune it: the way the string wants, and the way your piano does. They are not the same interval.

Phone speakers can't reproduce anything below about 500 Hz, so the low setting is silent-ish on one. The high setting moves everything up two octaves; same intervals, same errors, beats twice as fast and clearly audible on a handset.

Stripes stand still when the interval is pure. They crawl when it isn't.

The true fifth sits perfectly still. The piano's fifth wobbles: a slow pulse, roughly once a second. That pulse is two notes disagreeing about where they are. Once you hear it you can't unhear it, and I'm sorry.

01

Twelve fifths should come home. They don't.

Stack fifths and you eventually name every note: C, G, D, A, E, B, F♯, C♯, G♯, D♯, A♯, E♯. And E♯ is spelled the same as F, one more fifth lands on B♯, and B♯ is spelled the same as C. Twelve fifths, and you're home.

Except you aren't. Twelve pure fifths land past the octave. Not by a rounding error you can wave away; by a gap you can hear across a room.

The stack

Twelve pure fifths overshoot home by 23.46 cents, about a quarter of a semitone. The piano's fifths land exactly on it, because each one was shaved.

That overshoot has a name: the Pythagorean comma. And it isn't a mistake anyone made. It's arithmetic. A fifth is a 3:2 ratio; an octave is 2:1; and no number of 3s ever equals a number of 2s. The spiral is guaranteed never to close, forever, for the same reason 1/3 never finishes in decimal.

“Any point at which one might first settle down is arbitrary. There is no end or beginning, twelve fifths overshoot home and miss forever. As every note carries its octave within, the nature of the space is to fold, but never close.”
02

So we cheated, and we called it fair.

Equal temperament is the treaty. Take the gap, cut it into twelve equal pieces, and shave one piece off every fifth. Now the spiral closes. Every key works. Nothing is in tune, and nothing is more out of tune than anything else.

It is the most successful compromise in the history of music, and it is a compromise. Your piano cannot play a pure major third. It has never played one. It plays something 14 cents sharp of one and we've agreed to call that a third.

How wrong, exactly

Here's the part that matters later. The piano isn't wrong by one amount. Every interval is wrong by its own amount, and they're wildly different.

Beat rate is how fast the two versions pulse against each other, at this tuning. It runs from a lazy 0.4 per second to a nervous 8.6 per second, more than a twenty-fold spread.

Look at that last column for a second longer than feels necessary. Those are speeds. The piano's mistakes aren't a flat tax; they're a fan of different clocks, spread across more than an order of magnitude, and no two of them are simple multiples of each other.

A fan of incommensurate clocks is the most useful thing you can hand a physicist. That's the turn. Everything after this is what happens when you stop apologising for the errors and start using them.

03

Build the thing out of the errors.

Here is one machine. Eleven oscillators — eleven tuning forks, if you like — each one a pure interval from the table above. Each is detuned from the piano's version of itself by exactly the amount the piano gets it wrong. They're weakly coupled, so they can pull on each other.

04

Read it fast: it listens.

Feed a random signal into the bank and watch what sticks. Because the oscillators run at different speeds, each one holds a different slice of the recent past; the slow ones hold further back, the fast ones hold sharper. Train a readout on top and you can ask the bank questions about what it just heard. How well it answers is a number, and the number is measurable.

This is a real technique, called reservoir computing. You don't train the bank. You only train one linear layer that reads it. Everything hard is done by physics.

A bank like this can remember or it can mix, and it can't do both. Turn the coupling up and the oscillators start agreeing with each other. Agreement is what mixing is made of, and mixing is what lets a readout answer questions harder than what did you hear twenty steps ago. Sweep it and watch what that buys, and what it costs.

Uncoupled, the bank can be read three seconds back. At the top of the sweep it reaches less than one second, and mixing has gone up sevenfold. The exchange lives in reach, not in any single capacity number, and that's worth knowing before you measure one: count only the first thirty delays and you're looking through a six-tenths-of-a-second window at a memory three seconds long. The window hides the whole effect.

One number stays stubborn through all of it. Capacity never gets near thirty-four, which is its own ceiling: thirty-four state variables, and never more than half of them in use. The rest exist on paper and hold nothing new, because the oscillators are very nearly the same oscillator. Their frequencies differ by between 0.37 and 8.63 Hz out of 220.

Which is the comma again, wearing a different hat. The intervals are nearly right; that's the whole reason a piano nearly works. But nearly-right oscillators are nearly-redundant oscillators, and a bank of them holds far less than its size promises. The shortfall is the smallness of the error, measured this time in units of memory.

The comma isn't noise in this machine. It's the budget.

05

Read it slow: it remembers.

Now the same bank, read on a different clock. Instead of the instantaneous state, look at how far each oscillator has slipped from where it should be by now. That slippage is a number. Nudge it deliberately and it's a number you chose, which makes it storage.

Ten oscillator pairs, thirty-two possible positions each. Write something.

You can't hear a phase; the ear is nearly phase-deaf for steady tones, which Helmholtz worked out in the 1860s. So the register is read the way every tuner reads one: play it against a reference standing exactly where you wrote it. Matched slots reinforce and ring out. Drifted slots cancel themselves toward silence. Interference turns phase into loudness, and loudness the ear is extremely good at.

Leave the coupling at zero and it holds forever. Turn it up and the oscillators start persuading each other toward agreement, and as they agree, the differences between them vanish, and the message dissolves letter by letter into a single unified hum. Read it back as it goes and you hear voices drop out one at a time.

Watch which letters die first. The ones held by nearly-in-tune pairs go immediately; they had the least disagreement to spend. The ones held by the worst intervals, the ones the piano gets most catastrophically wrong, are the last to go. The more wrong the interval, the longer it can keep a secret.

06

One stone.

That was one machine the whole time. The fast reading listens; the slow reading remembers; and they are the same eleven oscillators, running at once, not interfering with each other, because they live on different clocks.

Which puts both readings on one knob. Turn the coupling up and the bank mixes harder, sevenfold across the sweep. Turn the coupling up and both of its memories go: the fast reading's reach falls from three seconds to under one, and the slow reading dissolves letter by letter as the oscillators talk themselves into agreement. The same agreement that makes the bank a better computer makes it a worse witness, on both clocks at once. There is no setting where you get everything, and the exchange rate is fixed by how badly the intervals disagree.

The error is not the price of the machine. The error is the machine. A bank of oscillators in perfect agreement is a single tuning fork: it computes nothing, remembers nothing, and has nothing to say. Everything it can do, it can do because it can't close.

“Harmony cultivates wanting by metabolizing our error as remainder. A music with no remainder leaves nothing to want in a world where wanting is the product.”

Which is also the whole of information theory, arrived at from the other side. A perfect prediction transmits nothing. A codec with no error tells you nothing about the world it was fitted to. The leftover is not what's left after the meaningful part; the leftover is the only part that was ever about anything.

Three hundred years ago some very practical people looked at a gap that could never be closed, split the difference twelve ways, and called it home. They thought they were papering over a flaw. They were building the only kind of instrument that can hold a thought.

What's real here. The audio is genuine additive synthesis at the stated frequencies; the beat rates you hear are the actual arithmetic, and they scale with the playback register, which is why the table's last column changes when you switch it. The reservoir is a bank of coupled damped resonators integrated live in your browser, with a ridge-regression readout fit on the spot; the capacity numbers are measured from that fit, not scripted, and its detunings stay pinned to concert pitch regardless of what you're listening at. Capacity is scored on 3,500 samples the readout never saw, fit on a different 3,500; with thirty-four features, in-sample scores carry a floor near 0.01 per task from fitting noise alone, which is enough to invent a result that isn't there. Reach is found by scanning past the edge of the chart until the held-out score dies, so it's a property of the bank rather than of the window. The oscillators are damped rather than self-sustaining, so what they hold is what you put in, and the linear part of each step is solved exactly instead of stepped, which is what keeps the fastest detunings stable. The storage register holds phase deviation from free-running, quantised to thirty-two levels per pair, and the read is real interference in the audio graph — two oscillators per slot, one at the current phase and one at the written phase, summing to 2·|cos(Δ/2)|. Nothing on this page is a mock-up.

What isn't claimed. This is not a better computer than the one you're reading it on. It's a demonstration that a physical system's imperfections can be its computational substrate, and that in this particular system the imperfection has a name musicians have used since Pythagoras.
