Tuning · Memory · Machines

Your piano
is a liar.

Every note on it is a little bit wrong. On purpose. It has been that way for three hundred years and nobody thought to mention it to you. This page is about that lie: how to hear it, why we signed up for it, and what a person can build out of the scraps it leaves on the floor.

Press a button. You'll need sound. A held note keeps sounding until you press the same button again, and the box in the corner shuts everything up at once. Every machine ought to come with one of those.

Two fifths

A fifth is C up to G, the friendliest interval in music after the octave. You can tune it two ways: the way the string wants it, or the way your piano has it. They are not the same interval.

A phone's speaker can't do much below about 500 Hz, so the low setting is mostly silence on a phone. The high setting moves everything up two octaves. Same intervals, same errors, and every beat runs four times as fast, which a phone can manage fine.

Stripes stand still when the interval is pure. They crawl when it isn't.

The true fifth sits there like a stone in a creek bed. The piano's fifth wobbles. It has a pulse you can count, and the band above clocks its speed at the register you're hearing. That pulse is two notes arguing about where they are. Once you've heard it you'll hear it in every piano for the rest of your life. Sorry about that.

01

Twelve fifths should bring you home. They don't.

Climb by fifths and sooner or later you name every note there is: C, G, D, A, E, B, F♯, C♯, G♯, D♯, A♯, E♯. E♯ is spelled the same as F, one more fifth lands on B♯, and B♯ is spelled the same as C. Twelve fifths and you're back where you started, seven octaves up, in time for supper.

Except you aren't. Twelve pure fifths overshoot. Not by some rounding error a committee could vote away. By a gap you can hear across a room.

The stack

Twelve pure fifths overshoot home by 23.46 cents, about a quarter of a semitone. The piano's fifths land on it exactly, because every one of them was shaved.

That overshoot has a name, the Pythagorean comma, and nobody made a mistake. It's arithmetic, as hard and as indifferent as sandstone. A fifth is a ratio of 3 to 2. An octave is 2 to 1. Multiply threes together as long as you like and you will never land on a power of two. The spiral cannot close. Not this year, not ever, for the same reason one-third never finishes as a decimal.

“Any point at which one might first settle down is arbitrary. There is no end or beginning, twelve fifths overshoot home and miss forever. As every note carries its octave within, the nature of the space is to fold, but never close.”
02

So we paved it over.

Equal temperament is the treaty. Take the gap, chop it into twelve equal pieces, and shave one piece off every fifth. Now the spiral closes. Every key works. Nothing is in tune, and nothing is any more out of tune than anything else. It's the interstate highway of music. It goes everywhere, it looks the same everywhere, and nobody ever pulls over to look at anything.

It is the most successful compromise in the history of music, and it is still a compromise. Your piano cannot play a pure major third. It never has. It plays something 14 cents sharp of one, and for three centuries we've agreed to call that a third and not make a fuss.

How wrong, exactly

Here's the part that matters later. The piano isn't wrong by one amount. Each interval is wrong by its own amount, and the amounts are all over the map.

Beat rate is how fast the two versions pulse against each other at the register you're listening at. Right now it runs from a lazy 0.37 per second to a jittery 8.63 per second, a 23-fold spread. Tap a row to hear one.

Look at that last column longer than seems reasonable. Those are speeds. The piano's mistakes are not a flat tax. They're a fan of clocks running at different rates across more than an order of magnitude, and no two of them divide evenly into each other.

A fan of clocks that never line up is about the most useful thing you can hand a physicist. Here the trail turns. Everything below is what happens when you quit apologizing for the errors and put them to work.

03

Build a machine out of the leftovers.

Here is one machine. Eleven oscillators, eleven tuning forks if you like, each one a pure interval from the table above. Each is detuned from the piano's version of itself by exactly the amount the piano gets it wrong. They're weakly coupled, so they can tug on each other the way neighbors do over a fence.

Play it. It's a chord made of everything the piano can't say.

04

Read it fast: it listens.

Feed the bank a random signal and see what sticks. The oscillators run at different speeds, so each one holds a different slice of the recent past. The slow ones hold further back, the fast ones hold it sharper. Put a readout on top and you can ask the bank what it just heard. How well it answers is a number, and you can measure it.

The trick is called reservoir computing. You don't train the bank at all. You train one thin linear layer that reads it, and physics does the heavy lifting, the way a river carves the canyon and the geologist only takes notes.

A bank like this can remember or it can mix. It can't do both. Turn the coupling up and the oscillators start agreeing with each other. Agreement is what mixing is made of, and mixing is what lets a readout answer harder questions than what did you hear twenty steps ago. Sweep it and watch what that buys and what it costs.

Left alone, the bank can be read almost three seconds back. At the top of the sweep it reaches one second, and mixing has gone up close to eightfold. The trade lives in reach, not in any single capacity number, and that's worth knowing before you go out measuring one. Count only the first thirty delays and you're peering through a six-tenths-of-a-second keyhole at a memory nearly three seconds long. The keyhole hides the whole business.

One number won't budge through any of it. Capacity never gets near thirty-four, which is its ceiling: thirty-four numbers the readout sees, and never more than half of them earning their keep. The rest are on the payroll and do nothing, because the oscillators are very nearly the same oscillator. Their frequencies differ by between 0.37 and 8.63 Hz out of 220.

Which is the comma again, in a different hat. The intervals are nearly right, and that's the whole reason a piano nearly works. But nearly right oscillators are nearly redundant oscillators, and a bank of them holds a good deal less than its size promises. The shortfall is the smallness of the error, measured this time in units of memory.

In this machine the comma is the budget.

05

Read it slow: it remembers.

Now the same bank, read on a slower clock. Skip the instantaneous state and look at how far each oscillator has slipped from where it ought to be by now. That slip is a number. Nudge it on purpose and it's a number you chose, and a number you chose is storage.

Ten oscillator pairs, thirty-two positions each. Write something. Keep it short. Ten letters is a telegram, not a novel.

Write into the drift

Ten characters, held as phase offsets between eleven oscillators.
K = 0.12

You can't hear a phase. The ear is nearly deaf to phase in a steady tone, as Helmholtz worked out in the 1860s. So the register gets read the way a tuner reads a string: against a reference standing exactly where you wrote it. Matched slots reinforce and ring out. Drifted slots cancel themselves down toward silence. Interference turns phase into loudness, and loudness the ear is very good at.

Leave the coupling at its default whisper and the message just about keeps. Four and a half minutes go by before the first slot slips, and the one that slips belongs to one of the most in-tune intervals on the page. Turn the knob up and the oscillators start persuading each other. As they come around to agreement, the differences the message was written in get pulled apart, and it dissolves a letter at a time. Read it back while it goes and you hear the voices drop out one by one.

Watch which letters die first. The ones held by nearly-in-tune pairs tend to go early. They had the least disagreement to spend. The ones held by the worst intervals, the ones the piano gets most outrageously wrong, hang on longest. The more wrong the interval, the longer it keeps a secret. I find that encouraging as a general principle.

06

One stone.

That was one machine the whole time. The fast reading listens and the slow reading remembers, and they're the same eleven oscillators running at once, minding their own business, because they live on different clocks.

Which puts both readings on one knob, and on this page it is literally one knob: every coupling slider above is the same control, driving the same bank. Turn it up and the bank mixes harder, close to eightfold across the sweep. Turn it up and both of its memories go. The fast reading's reach falls from three seconds to under one, and the slow reading dissolves letter by letter as the oscillators talk themselves into agreement. The same agreement that makes the bank a better computer makes it a worse witness, on both clocks at once. There's no setting where you get everything, and the exchange rate is fixed by how badly the intervals disagree.

The error is the machine. A bank of oscillators in perfect agreement is a single tuning fork. It computes nothing, remembers nothing, and has nothing to say. Everything it can do, it does because it can't close. Wild country works the same way, if you ask me. The part nobody got around to tidying up is the only part still worth the walk.

“Harmony cultivates wanting by metabolizing our error as remainder. A music with no remainder leaves nothing to want in a world where wanting is the product.”
07

The leftover is the message.

Come at the same idea from the other side of the mountain and you land in Claude Shannon's office at Bell Labs in 1948. Shannon wanted to know how much a message tells you. His answer was so simple it sounds like a joke. A message tells you exactly as much as it surprises you.

If I tell you the sun came up this morning, I've told you nothing. You'd have bet the ranch on it. If I tell you it came up in the west, that's news. Shannon put a number on it: an event with odds of one in two is worth one bit, one in four is two bits, one in a thousand is about ten. Something you were certain of is worth zero. A message you could have predicted perfectly carries no information at all. It's a postcard from the town you already live in.

Every practical way of shipping signals around takes that literally. The codec in your phone doesn't send your voice. It guesses what your voice will do next, sends only how wrong the guess was, and the phone at the other end runs the same guess and adds the correction back. Video works the same way, frame after frame. If the guess were perfect there'd be nothing to send, and the receiver would learn nothing. The correction is the whole cargo.

Equal temperament is a guess everybody shares. Every tuner on earth knows where a textbook piano's strings ought to sit. So a piano tells you something only where it departs from the textbook. Here is one, sent by mail three ways.

The first bar sends every key's pitch from scratch. The second sends only how far each key sits from the textbook, because the receiver already owns the textbook. The third goes one better and sends only how far each key differs from the key before it, because a damp summer moves neighbors together. Each smarter guess costs fewer bits. Pull the slider to zero and the second and third bars drop to nothing. A piano tuned exactly to the book can be mailed for free, and you'd learn nothing about it from the letter. It has no story, because it has no error.

That's the tuning channel in the notes, which works out that a piano's departures from the book could hide about 300 bits before an audience caught on. It's also the oscillator bank, seen through Shannon's spectacles. Three of his ideas do the work.

Copies don't count. Two newspapers printing the same story don't tell you twice as much. Information is what one source holds that the others don't. Eleven oscillators that agree are one oscillator wearing eleven hats, which is exactly what the budget panel measures: shrink the errors to zero and the bank holds 1.08 of its 34, one oscillator's worth, because every channel has become a copy of every other. The detunings are what keep the copies apart.

Infinite isn't the same as roomy. The spiral of fifths never closes, so it visits infinitely many pitches. Why not store infinitely much in it? Because every reader has a noise floor, and Shannon's capacity counts the states you can tell apart above the noise, not the states that exist. Points crowded closer than your ear or your strobe can split are one point. The register uses thirty-two levels per slot because that's what drift and an eye can resolve. A computer's arithmetic could hold millions. No instrument could.

Agreement is forgetting. When the bank's phases are scattered, a great many arrangements are possible, and each one could mean something. Couple them and they crowd together. The spread of possible states shrinks, and the number of messages it can tell apart shrinks with it. Turning up the coupling pours bits out of the register as surely as opening a drain. The same knob that buys mixing spends memory, and Shannon's arithmetic is why the exchange can't be dodged.

So the leftover isn't what's left once the meaningful part is gone. The leftover is the only part that was ever about anything. Three hundred years ago some very practical people looked at a gap that could never be closed, split the difference twelve ways, and called it home. They figured they were papering over a flaw. They were building the only kind of instrument that can hold a thought.

08

Past the end of the pavement.

The road ends here. Beyond it is the back country, eight more pages where the same idea goes walking: the comma's cousins in calendars and sunflowers, a contest between historical tunings over which one thinks best, metronomes falling into step on a plank, fireflies doing it in the dark, a letter hidden in a piano's tuning, a test of your own ears, an instrument you play by hand, and a comodulogram waiting for a sunflower to plug into it.

Where the numbers come from. The audio is additive synthesis at the stated frequencies; the beat rates you hear are the frequency differences themselves, and they scale with the playback register, which is why the table's last column changes when you switch it. The reservoir is a bank of coupled damped resonators integrated live in your browser, with a ridge-regression readout fit on the spot; the capacity numbers come out of that fit, and its detunings stay pinned to concert pitch regardless of what you're listening at. Capacity is scored on 3,500 samples the readout never saw, fit on a different 3,500; with thirty-four features, in-sample scores carry a floor near 0.01 per task from fitting noise alone, which is enough to invent a result that isn't there. Reach is found by scanning past the edge of the chart until the held-out score dies, so it's a property of the bank rather than of the window. The budget panel reruns the entire measurement with every detuning multiplied by one factor; at zero the bank is eleven copies of one tuning fork and holds 1.08 of its ceiling of 34. The oscillators are damped rather than self-sustaining, so what they hold is what you put in, and the linear part of each step is solved exactly instead of stepped, which is what keeps the fastest detunings stable. The storage register holds phase deviation from free-running, quantised to thirty-two levels per pair, and the read is interference in the audio graph — two oscillators per slot, one at the current phase and one at the written phase, summing to 2·|cos(Δ/2)|. Its decay is the same mean-field coupling, integrated while you watch. Nothing schedules which letter dies; the detunings decide. The mailed piano is a seeded random tuning; each scheme's cost is 88 keys times log2 of the number of half-cent levels its residuals span, a simple fixed-length code and an upper bound on what a cleverer code would need.

The size of the claim. This is not a better computer than the one you're reading it on. It's a demonstration that a physical system's imperfections can be its computational substrate, and that in this particular system the imperfection has a name musicians have used since Pythagoras.
