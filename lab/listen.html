The Listening Test

The tuning-channel note estimates that a piano's tuning can hide about 290 bits of data before an audience notices. That figure rests on two listening thresholds that were estimated, not measured. This page measures them with your ears.

Each trial plays the same sound twice. One of the two has been altered, and you pick which one. The test takes about ten minutes. Use a quiet room, and headphones if you have them. Nothing leaves this browser unless you send it yourself.

  1. Set up your sound and do two practice trials (section 01).
  2. Run Test A, an altered tuning (section 02).
  3. Run Test B, a spread unison (section 03).
  4. Read your results, and download them if you want to share them (section 04).

02

Test A: an altered tuning

You hear a five-chord sequence (C, A minor, F, G, C) twice. One run uses equal temperament, the standard piano tuning that splits the octave into twelve equal semitones. In the other run, six of the twelve note names are raised by Δ cents and the other six are lowered by Δ. A cent is a hundredth of a semitone. Which six go up is chosen at random each trial.

  1. Press Start test A. The two runs play back to back. The First and Second buttons light up while each run plays.
  2. Press First (key 1) or Second (key 2) for the run that sounded altered. You can answer once the second run starts.
  3. Press Replay (space) to hear the pair again. If you're unsure, take your best guess.
  4. Keep answering. The test ends on its own, after at most 40 trials.

The size of Δ adjusts to your answers. Two right answers in a row make it smaller, and one wrong answer makes it larger. This method is called a staircase. Over the run, Δ settles at the size you pick correctly about 71 percent of the time. That size is your threshold.

03

Test B: a spread unison

In the middle and upper range of a piano, each note has three strings. They are normally tuned to the same pitch, which is called a unison. A tuner can set them slightly apart, and the note sounds a little fuller, the way a choir sounds fuller than one voice. The note assumes a spread of ±1.5 cents is heard as warmth, not as a mistake.

You hear the note A twice. In one, all three strings are exactly together. In the other, one string is Δ cents flat, one is in tune, and one is Δ cents sharp. Pick the one that was spread. The buttons, keys and staircase work the same as in Test A.

Listen to the end of each note, not the attack. Close pitches produce beats, a slow rise and fall in loudness. Here the beats show up as the sound fades.

04

Your results

This section feeds your two thresholds into the note's formulas. The result is the channel size: how many bits a tuning could hide, where one bit is one yes-or-no choice. Until you finish a test, the note's estimate is used in its place.

The channel has three parts. Temperament comes from Test A. Unisons come from Test B. Per-key stretch is a small offset on each of the 88 keys. It sits on top of the usual stretch curve, which tunes high notes slightly sharp and low notes slightly flat. This test does not measure it.

Treat your numbers as a lower bound. Here you know one of two sounds is altered, you hear them back to back, and you can replay them. A concert audience has none of that help, so it would need larger alterations before noticing. The piano can probably hide more than the figure shown.

05

Measure a real piano

If you have a piano nearby, you can measure how far one of its intervals is from pure. An interval is two notes played together. It is pure when the two frequencies form an exact whole-number ratio, such as 3:2 for a fifth.

A piano note is a mix of partials: the main pitch plus fainter tones at about 2, 3, 4 and more times its frequency. When a partial of the low note nearly matches a partial of the high note, the two beat. For a fifth, the low note's third partial meets the high note's second. The beat rate gives the tuning error. The strings' own behavior adds some uncertainty.

The page measures the loudness of the microphone signal 100 times a second. This record is the loudness envelope. The page finds a slow, steady pulse in it and converts the pulse rate into cents.

  1. Choose the interval and the lower note below.
  2. Press Listen through the microphone and allow access.
  3. Strike both notes, hold the keys down, and stay quiet for about ten seconds.
  4. Read the beat rate and the distance from pure. The confidence figure shows how clear the pulse was.

No piano? Press Test with a synthetic piano. It runs the meter on a computer-made dyad (two notes sounded together) whose beat rate is known exactly. Hear the synthetic dyad plays it aloud.

What was measured, and how. Test A plays C–Am–F–G–C voiced C3 E4 G4 C5, A2 E4 A4 C5, F2 F4 A4 C5, G2 D4 G4 B4, C3 E4 G4 C5. Chords start 0.8 s apart and the last rings for 1.9 s. Each note is a Kit.sound.pluck: six partials, each slightly sharp by the factor 1 + 0.0004h² (a small stiff-string stretch), with exponential decay. The altered run moves a random six of the twelve pitch classes up by Δ and the other six down by Δ. Seven pitch classes appear in the progression, so at least one always moves against the rest, and the alteration is never a plain transposition. Every altered pitch class sits at the edge of a ±Δ window, so some intervals are off by as much as 2Δ. Test B plays A4 (440 Hz, or 1760 Hz on the phone setting) as three 3.4 s plucks struck together in phase, at 0, 0, 0 or −Δ, 0, +Δ cents.

Both tests are two-interval forced choice, with the altered sound's position random. Each uses a 2-down-1-up staircase (Levitt, 1971), which converges near 70.7% correct. Δ starts at 12 cents (A) or 4 cents (B). It moves by a factor of 1.5, then 1.2 from the second reversal on, and is capped at 40 or 16 cents. A run stops at 8 reversals or 40 trials. The threshold is the geometric mean of Δ at the last six reversals. A run that ends with fewer than two reversals reports the geometric mean of its last six levels and is flagged as unconverged.

A forced choice measures discrimination: an alert listener, a reference next to the target, and unlimited replays. An audience has none of these, so these thresholds sit below the audience detection window the note asks about, and the recomputed channel is conservative. Results also depend on the speaker or headphones, the register, the room, the level, practice and fatigue, and on one progression in one key. A real piano's partials are louder, longer and more inharmonic than these synthetic ones. One listener's results do not generalize.

The channel uses the note's own formulas with your thresholds rounded to 0.01 cent. Temperament: levels = ⌊2A/2⌋ + 1 at the note's 2-cent strobe-tuner resolution, bits = 12·log2(levels). Per-key stretch: not measured here. The page takes the note's 88·log23 ≈ 139.5 bits and scales it linearly by A/5, capped at 1, because a per-key offset should be no larger than the temperament window it sits inside. A finer threshold can shrink this term but never grow it. Unisons: levels = ⌊2B/1⌋ + 1 at 1-cent resolution, two strings by 60 notes, halved for stability. The note's inputs (5 and 1.5 cents) give 31.0 + 139.5 + 120.0 = 290.5 bits.

The beat meter takes a 40 ms Hann-weighted RMS every 10 ms. It starts 0.25 s after the loudest point in the window (the strike). It divides the envelope by a quadratic fit to its logarithm to remove the decay, applies a Hann window, and evaluates a direct Fourier transform from max(0.2 Hz, 1.5/T) to 20 Hz on a grid no coarser than min(0.02, 1/4T) Hz, then refines the tallest peak. T is the analyzed length. Confidence is the share of the band's power within ±2/T of the peak, times min(1, cycles/3), times min(1, depth/0.2%), where depth is the beat's modulation depth. It is cut to 30% for a peak pinned to the bottom of the band. The cents figure assumes harmonic partials, c = 1200·log2(1 + b/(p·flow)), and treats the lower note as sitting at equal-tempered pitch with A4 = 440 Hz. Beats can't tell sharp from flat. Real strings are stiff and their partials run sharp, which shifts the coincident partials and so the beat rate, more at the ends of the keyboard. Browsers may process the microphone signal even when asked not to. The synthetic test runs the same estimator on a dyad whose beat is known exactly.
