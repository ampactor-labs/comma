Lab · 04 · Pulse-coupled oscillators

Fireflies

Some fireflies flash in time with each other. A whole hillside goes dark, then lights at once, with no leader. This page shows how one simple rule produces that, and what the swarm loses when it agrees.

Each firefly here is a clock that flashes once per cycle. When it sees another firefly flash, it moves its own clock forward a little. That is the only interaction. Below, you can watch a meadow of these clocks fall into step, measure how much information disappears as they do, and run the same rule on two real phones that hear each other through the air.

01

How the fireflies fall into step

Set the pull above zero and watch the meadow. The flashes start out scattered, gather into clumps, and the clumps merge until every firefly flashes together.

Each firefly follows one rule. It carries a charge that climbs toward a threshold, quickly at first and then more slowly. When the charge reaches the threshold, the firefly flashes and the charge resets to zero. When it sees another firefly flash, its charge jumps up by a fixed step, called the pull (ε). If that jump carries it past the threshold, it flashes at once and resets in the same instant as the firefly that triggered it. The two then start their next cycle together. A firefly's phase is how far it is through its cycle, from 0 just after a flash to 1 at the next one.

Renato Mirollo and Steven Strogatz proved in 1990 that a group of identical fireflies built this way, each one seeing all the others, ends up flashing as one from almost any starting state. The slowing climb is what makes it work. Near the threshold, a step in charge is worth a large jump in time, so fireflies that are almost ready get pulled into the flash. Near the bottom, the same step is worth very little time, so a firefly that has just flashed is hardly moved away from the group.

These fireflies are not identical. Their natural rates come from the piano on the main page. Eleven of its intervals are slightly out of tune, so each one beats: two close tones make a slow pulse. Those beat rates run from 0.37 to 8.63 Hz (pulses per second). The page maps them evenly onto a range of firefly rates centred on one flash per second, plus or minus the spread you set. The slowest beat becomes the slowest firefly. With more than eleven fireflies, the same eleven rates repeat in order.

02

Agreement erases information

Watch the gauge while the swarm locks. It falls toward zero. A scattered swarm holds information in its differences, and falling into step uses those differences up.

The gauge measures how many bits it takes to say where every firefly's clock stands, with the dial cut into 32 slices. Fireflies spread evenly over all 32 slices give 5 bits, the most a 32-slice dial can show. A swarm smaller than 32 gives less, because it cannot fill every slice. As the fireflies agree, the number falls. When they all flash together it reaches zero, because knowing one firefly's clock tells you every other one. The whole swarm then tells you no more than a single firefly could: lit or dark.

The main page shows the same effect. There, a message is stored in the small offsets between oscillators, and turning up the coupling (how strongly they pull on each other) erases it, starting with the most closely tuned pairs. Here, the offsets are the scattered phases before the swarm locks, and the pull uses them up. The locked swarm is easy to see but stores nothing. The information was in the scatter, including the stragglers that have not joined yet.

The gauge does not stay at zero. When the spread is above zero, the faster fireflies pull ahead between flashes, so the phases drift apart a little during each cycle. The next flash pulls them back together. In the chart this shows up as a sawtooth that repeats about once a second: the piano's spread of rates reappearing every cycle.

03

Sync two phones by sound

This part runs the same rule on real devices. Two phones with no network connection between them fall into step using only sound.

Each phone acts as one firefly. On each flash it lights its screen and plays a short chirp, 40 ms long at 3 kHz. Its natural period is picked at random near one second. Between chirps it listens through the microphone for that pitch. It ignores what it hears just after its own chirp, so it does not react to itself. When it hears the other phone, it moves its clock forward by the same rule the fireflies use.

Try it with two phones

  1. Open this page on both phones. Phones need the page served over https before the browser will open the microphone (see the notes at the bottom).
  2. Turn the volume up on both phones.
  3. Place the phones about a meter apart in a quiet room. They do not use Wi-Fi or Bluetooth.
  4. Press Join as a firefly on each phone. Allow the microphone when the browser asks.
  5. Listen. At first the two chirps come at different times. Over a while they move toward each other and land together, or with one phone slightly behind.
  6. Look at the ring around the glow on each screen. Blue ticks gather near the top once the phones agree.
  7. To stop, press Leave, the Stop button in the corner, or Escape.

Try it with one phone

Press Practice with an imaginary partner. This runs a second firefly inside the page with its own period. Each of the two hears the other's chirp about 70 ms late, and each chirp has a 1 in 20 chance of being missed. The partner's chirp is lower and quieter, so you can hear the two close in on each other. The microphone stays off in practice mode.

The model. Each firefly has a phase φ in [0, 1) that advances at its natural rate. Its state is x = ln(1 + (eb−1)φ)/b with b = 3. This curve is concave (it rises fast, then slowly), as the Mirollo–Strogatz result requires. At x = 1 the firefly flashes and resets. When a firefly sees a flash, x rises by ε divided by the number of fireflies it can see, unless it flashed within the last 150 ms. So ε is the total jump it would get if all of them flashed at once. A firefly pushed to x = 1 flashes in that same instant and resets together with the one that pushed it (absorption). Natural rates are 1 Hz × (1 ± spread). The piano's eleven beat rates are mapped linearly onto that range, slowest beat to slowest firefly, and fireflies past the eleventh reuse the same rates in order. The swarm is integrated in 5 ms steps. Whole meadow is instant all-to-all coupling, the case the 1990 proof covers for identical oscillators. With a spread of rates nothing is guaranteed, so the locking you see here is measured, not proved. Neighbours only adds a 50 ms response delay so the waves are visible. The information gauge is the plug-in entropy of a 32-bin histogram of phases. Its ceiling is log2 32 = 5 bits, or log2 n for n < 32 fireflies (3.46 bits for eleven), and it reads low for small swarms. The order parameter is r = |⟨e2πiφ⟩|. “In step” is when the 2-second average of r first rises above 0.95. It resets if that average falls below 0.8.

Real fireflies are more complicated. Photinus carolinus, in the Great Smoky Mountains, flashes in bursts of several quick flashes separated by long dark gaps, and what synchronises is mostly the bursts. Field recordings suggest that a lone male flashes irregularly and the rhythm appears only in a group. The mangrove fireflies of Southeast Asia (Pteroptyx) seem to adjust their own period and can delay a flash as well as advance it. Pulse coupling with a fixed period does neither. This page uses the pulse-coupling idea, not the biology.

The microphone. The page asks for audio with echo cancellation, noise suppression and automatic gain control turned off, so that it can hear the other phone. As a result it also hears itself. An analyser takes a 1024-point spectrum, about 21 ms of sound at 48 kHz, once per animation frame. A chirp counts when the 2.8–3.2 kHz band is 12 dB above a noise floor and 6 dB above the bands on either side. The noise floor follows quiet frames with a 1.5 s time constant, and a steady tone lasting more than 0.4 s is absorbed into it. Anything heard from 30 ms before to 350 ms after the phone's own chirp is ignored, and at most one detection counts per 150 ms. A whistle, a squeaky hinge or a smoke-alarm chirp near 3 kHz can cause a false detection. A loud or large room can stop the phones hearing each other. The page does not correct for latency. The speaker path, the microphone path and the once-per-frame check each add delay, and the total varies by device: likely tens of milliseconds, up to a couple of hundred on some phones. A locked pair therefore settles with the follower late by about that much. Some phones lower the speaker volume while the microphone is open, so turn the volume up.

Opening the file. Browsers open the microphone only on a secure page. Desktop Chrome treats a file opened from disk as secure, so the two-phone mode works from file:// there. Phones need the page served over https. Practice mode needs no microphone anywhere. Everything else runs offline from the file, with no network requests.
