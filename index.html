Tuning · Memory · Machines

Every note on a piano is slightly out of tune.

On purpose. This page shows you how to hear it, why pianos are tuned that way, and how the leftover errors can be used to build a small machine that remembers what it hears.

You'll need sound. Buttons that hold a note keep playing until you press them again. The box in the bottom-right corner stops everything, and so does the Escape key.

Two fifths

A fifth is the interval from C up to G. In a pure fifth, the higher string vibrates exactly 3 times for every 2 vibrations of the lower one. A piano's fifth is slightly smaller than that. Play both and compare.

Phone speakers can't play much below about 500 Hz, so Phone speaker moves everything up two octaves. The intervals and their errors stay the same, and every beat runs four times as fast. Headphones plays at concert pitch.

The stripes stand still when an interval is pure and move when it beats.

The pure fifth sounds steady. The piano's fifth has a slow, regular pulse, called a beat. Beats happen whenever two tones are close but not equal in frequency: their waves drift in and out of step, so the sound swells and fades. The beat rate equals the difference between the two frequencies, and the striped band moves at that rate.

01

Twelve fifths don't add up to seven octaves.

Start on C and go up by a fifth twelve times: C, G, D, A, E, B, F♯, C♯, G♯, D♯, A♯, E♯, B♯. You pass through every note on the keyboard and land on B♯, which is the same key as C, seven octaves higher. That's the theory.

In practice, twelve pure fifths overshoot seven octaves by a small but clearly audible amount. Press the buttons to hear the climb and where it lands.

The stack

Twelve pure fifths overshoot the starting note by 23.46 cents, about a quarter of a semitone. Twelve piano fifths land on it exactly, because each one is made slightly narrow.

The overshoot is called the Pythagorean comma. It measures 23.46 cents, where a cent is one hundredth of a piano semitone. It comes from simple arithmetic. Twelve fifths multiply the frequency by (3/2)12 ≈ 129.75. Seven octaves multiply it by 27 = 128. A power of 3 can never equal a power of 2, so no number of pure fifths ever lands exactly on an octave.

“Any point at which one might first settle down is arbitrary. There is no end or beginning, twelve fifths overshoot home and miss forever. As every note carries its octave within, the nature of the space is to fold, but never close.”
02

Equal temperament spreads the error around.

Modern pianos use equal temperament. The comma is split into twelve equal pieces, and every fifth is made one piece smaller, 1.96 cents narrow. Now twelve fifths close the circle exactly, and music sounds equally good in every key. The cost is that almost no interval is pure.

The major third shows the cost most clearly. A pure major third has a frequency ratio of 5 to 4. The piano's major third is 14 cents wider than that, and every piano has been built that way for about three hundred years.

That matters because a set of oscillators running at unrelated speeds is a good starting point for a machine that stores and processes information. The rest of the page builds one.

03

A machine built from the errors.

The machine is a bank of eleven oscillators. Picture each one as a pendulum swinging at one interval's beat rate, from 0.37 to 8.63 times a second. Each one slowly loses energy, so it needs input to keep moving. They are also weakly coupled: each oscillator is pulled a little toward the average of all eleven.

Press Play the bank to hear the eleven pure intervals together.

Coupling, K, sets how strongly each oscillator is pulled toward the group average. At low coupling each keeps its own time. At high coupling they fall into step, and anything stored in their differences is lost. This slider controls the same bank in every section below.
04

Read it fast: the bank as a computer.

Send a random signal into the bank. Because the oscillators turn at different speeds, the input leaves a different trace in each one, and each trace fades over about two seconds. So at any moment the bank's state holds a blurred record of what it heard recently.

Reservoir computing uses exactly this. The oscillators are never adjusted. Only a simple readout is trained: a weighted sum of the bank's 34 state values, fitted to answer questions such as "what was the input 20 steps ago?" Its answers are scored on data it was not trained on.

Memory and mixing trade off against each other. Mixing means combining past inputs, for example multiplying the input from one step ago by the input from three steps ago. More coupling makes the oscillators agree more, which helps mixing and shortens memory.

With no coupling, the bank can be read back almost three seconds. At the strongest coupling it reaches one second, while mixing rises close to eightfold. If you count only the first 30 delays, 0.6 seconds, you miss this trade-off, because the memory being lost lies further back. That is why the test keeps probing past the edge of the chart.

Capacity never gets near its ceiling of 34, the number of values the readout sees. At most about half of them are useful. The reason is that the oscillators are very similar: their speeds differ by only 0.37 to 8.63 Hz, out of 220, so much of what they hold is duplicated.

This links back to the comma. The piano's intervals are nearly right, which is why the piano sounds acceptable. But nearly identical oscillators carry nearly identical information. The size of the tuning errors sets how much the bank can hold.

05

Read it slow: the bank as storage.

The same oscillators can also store a message. Each oscillator's phase can be nudged ahead of or behind where it would otherwise be. That offset stays put as long as nothing disturbs it, so it can hold a value.

Ten oscillators each hold one character, using 32 possible offsets: 5 bits each, 50 bits in all. The eleventh oscillator is the reference. Type up to ten characters and press Write it in.

Write into the drift

Ten characters, held as phase offsets between eleven oscillators.
K = 0.12

You can't hear phase directly; the ear barely notices it in a steady tone, as Helmholtz showed in the 1860s. So the page reads the register the way a piano tuner checks a string: it plays each slot against a reference tone set to the written value. If the slot still matches, the two tones reinforce and ring loudly. If it has drifted, they partly cancel and the slot goes quiet. Interference turns phase into loudness, and the ear judges loudness well.

At the default coupling of 0.12 the message holds: the first slot slips after about four and a half minutes. Press Let it decay to raise the coupling to 2. The oscillators pull each other into step, the stored offsets are erased, and the letters fail one at a time. Read it back while it decays and you hear the voices drop out.

Watch which letters fail first. Slots held by the most accurate intervals tend to go early, because their oscillators are slow and closest in speed to the group, so they are the easiest to pull into step. Slots held by the intervals the piano gets most wrong hold their letters longest.

06

One bank, two uses.

Both readings use the same eleven oscillators at the same time. The fast reading, the computer, uses their moment-to-moment state. The slow reading, the storage, uses their accumulated phase offsets. They don't interfere, because they work on different timescales.

Coupling affects both, and every coupling slider on this page is the same control. More coupling makes the bank mix about eightfold better. It also cuts reach from three seconds to under one and erases the stored message. More agreement between the oscillators makes a better mixer and a worse memory, in both readings at once. How much you trade is set by how different the oscillators' speeds are, which is set by the tuning errors.

The errors are what make the machine work. With perfect tuning, all eleven oscillators would be identical and would act as one. The bank would remember nothing and compute nothing.

“Harmony cultivates wanting by metabolizing our error as remainder. A music with no remainder leaves nothing to want in a world where wanting is the product.”
07

Why this is information theory.

The same idea sits at the center of Claude Shannon's information theory, published in 1948. Shannon defined the information in a message as how surprising it is. An event with a 1-in-2 chance carries 1 bit. A 1-in-4 event carries 2 bits, and a 1-in-1,000 event about 10 bits. An event you were certain of carries 0 bits. A message you could have predicted perfectly tells you nothing.

Everyday compression is built on this. A phone's voice codec predicts what your voice will do next and sends only the prediction error. The receiving phone makes the same prediction and adds the error back. Video works the same way, frame by frame. The better the prediction, the less there is to send. The prediction error is the information.

Tuning works the same way. Everyone knows the textbook tuning, so a piano only tells you something new where it differs from the textbook. The panel below shows this with numbers.

The first bar sends each key's pitch from scratch. The second sends only each key's difference from the textbook, because the receiver already knows the textbook. The third sends only how much each key differs from the previous key, because neighboring keys drift together. Each better assumption costs fewer bits. Move the slider to zero: the piano now matches the textbook exactly, and the second and third methods need 0 bits. There is nothing new to send.

The tuning-channel notes apply this to a real piano and estimate that its departures from textbook tuning could hide about 300 bits before listeners noticed. Three more of Shannon's ideas explain the oscillator bank.

Duplicates add nothing. Two copies of a message carry the same information as one. Eleven identical oscillators hold what one holds, which is why capacity falls to 1.08 out of 34 when the errors are scaled to zero. The tuning errors keep the oscillators different from each other.

Resolution limits capacity. The spiral of fifths never closes, so in principle it contains infinitely many distinct pitches. But capacity depends on how many states you can tell apart above the noise, not on how many exist. The register uses 32 levels per slot because drift and a visual reading can reliably tell 32 apart. The computer's arithmetic could represent millions; no real instrument could read them.

Agreement erases information. When the phases are spread out, many arrangements are possible, and each can stand for a different message. Coupling pulls the phases together, which shrinks the number of arrangements that can be told apart, and with it the number of bits the register can hold. That is why turning up coupling helps mixing but costs memory.

Equal temperament was designed to hide the comma, but the error never went away. It was spread across every interval. Those leftover errors are exactly what lets the bank hold information.

08

More experiments.

The lab takes the idea further on eight more pages: the comma's equivalents in calendars, planets and sunflowers; a comparison of historical tunings by how much their errors let the bank remember; metronomes falling into step on a moving plank; fireflies synchronizing; a message hidden in a piano's tuning; a test of how small a mistuning you can hear; an instrument that lets you drive the bank by hand; and a tool for finding cross-rhythm coupling in plant recordings.

Where the numbers come from. The audio is additive synthesis at the stated frequencies; the beat rates you hear are the frequency differences themselves, and they scale with the playback register, which is why the table's last column changes when you switch it. The reservoir is a bank of coupled damped resonators integrated live in your browser, with a ridge-regression readout fit on the spot; the capacity numbers come out of that fit, and its detunings stay pinned to concert pitch regardless of what you're listening at. Capacity is scored on 3,500 samples the readout never saw, fit on a different 3,500; with thirty-four features, in-sample scores carry a floor near 0.01 per task from fitting noise alone, which is enough to invent a result that isn't there. Reach is found by scanning past the edge of the chart until the held-out score dies, so it's a property of the bank rather than of the window. The budget panel reruns the entire measurement with every detuning multiplied by one factor; at zero the bank is eleven copies of one oscillator and holds 1.08 of its ceiling of 34. The oscillators are damped rather than self-sustaining, so what they hold is what you put in, and the linear part of each step is solved exactly instead of stepped, which is what keeps the fastest detunings stable. The storage register holds phase deviation from free-running, quantised to thirty-two levels per pair, and the read is interference in the audio graph: two oscillators per slot, one at the current phase and one at the written phase, summing to 2·|cos(Δ/2)|. Its decay is the same mean-field coupling, integrated while you watch. Nothing schedules which letter fails; the detunings decide. The mailed piano is a seeded random tuning; each method's cost is 88 keys times log2 of the number of half-cent levels its residuals span, a simple fixed-length code and an upper bound on what a cleverer code would need.

The size of the claim. This is not a better computer than the one you're reading it on. It's a demonstration that a physical system's imperfections can be its computational substrate, and that in this particular system the imperfection has a name musicians have used since Pythagoras. On one real task it holds up: given recordings of spoken digits as nothing but loudness, a readout alone names 26% of them correctly, the piano's bank lifts that to 50%, and the same bank with its errors shrunk to zero falls back to 26% (tools/digits.mjs, on the Free Spoken Digit Dataset). Given eleven frequency bands instead, the readout reaches 91% without the bank's help.
