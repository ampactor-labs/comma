Metronomes on a Plank

Eleven metronomes sit on a wooden plank. Each one ticks at a slightly different tempo. With the plank on the floor, each keeps its own time. Put the plank on two soda cans so it can roll, and the metronomes fall into step in well under half a minute. The plank causes this: every swing pushes it a little, and its motion pushes back on all of them at once. This page writes a message into the small timing differences between the metronomes, then shows the plank erasing it.

The plank on rollers is a physical version of the Coupling slider on the main page. An oscillator is anything that repeats a cycle, like a pendulum. Coupling means each oscillator's motion pushes on the others.

01

Watch the metronomes fall into step

How to use it. The page opens with the plank on rollers and the word METRONOMES already written, one letter per metronome. The leftmost metronome, marked with a diamond, carries no letter. Watch the letters turn from cyan to amber as they slip. Then watch the arms gradually swing together. Next, press On the floor, then Reset, then Write, and leave it running. With the plank on the floor, the message holds for as long as you watch.

The first slider sets how freely the plank moves. Its readout is the mass ratio: the metronomes' total mass divided by the mass of metronomes and plank together. Zero means the plank sits on the floor and cannot move. The second slider sets how far apart the slowest and fastest metronomes run. Each metronome's tempo comes from one musical interval, named on the plank's front edge (P5 is a perfect fifth, M3 a major third, h7 the harmonic seventh, M2 a major second, M7 a major seventh; the others are numbered harmonics). A piano plays each interval slightly out of tune, so the two notes beat: they make a slow pulse, like two guitar strings slightly out of tune. The faster an interval beats on a piano, the faster its metronome runs.

Phase wheel

Phase is where a metronome is in its swing, drawn as an angle. One full swing, there and back, is one trip around the circle. The circle turns at the metronomes' average rate, so a metronome running at the average tempo stays in place. Each solid dot is a metronome. Each hollow ring is its ghost: a copy that runs on a fixed plank, unaffected by the others. The angle from ghost to dot is the letter, and the inner arcs show that angle, one arc per metronome. The 32 notches around the edge are the 32 possible letters. Larger dots belong to intervals that are further out of tune on a piano. The arrow from the center is the mean field, the average of all the phases.

Agreement and memory

R, the order parameter, measures how closely the arms agree. It is 1 when every arm swings together and near 0 when they are scattered. It equals the length of the arrow on the phase wheel. The dashed line is R = 0.95, which this page counts as in step. Letters intact counts the letters that have not slipped since you pressed Write.

02

How the plank couples the metronomes

The main page has a Coupling slider, labeled K. Turning it up pulls eleven oscillators toward their average, called the mean field. As they come into agreement, the message stored in them comes apart one letter at a time. A plank on rollers does the same thing physically, with nothing programmed in.

Each swing of a metronome pushes the plank slightly sideways. The plank moves by an amount set by all eleven swings added together. Every metronome then feels that motion as a small push at its base. That shared push is the mean field.

On the floor, the plank cannot move, so there is no shared push. This is the same as K = 0. Each metronome keeps its own time and its own letter. On rollers, the plank's push draws the metronomes toward the same phase, the way a crowd's clapping drifts into one rhythm. Turn on the ticks to hear it: the scattered ticks close up into a single click.

The message is not stored in any single metronome. It is stored in how far each metronome has moved from where it would be if it ran alone, which is its ghost. Agreement pulls every metronome to the same phase. Those distances shrink to zero, and the message goes with them. The more the metronomes agree, the less information their differences can hold.

03

The letters slip before the arms line up

The arms take a while to line up. The letters are lost much sooner. Each letter owns one of 32 slots around the circle, each 11.25 degrees wide. A letter slips once its phase drifts more than half a slot, about 5.6 degrees, from where it was written. A plank that barely seems to move can push a metronome that far within a few swings. The slip log under the plank records when each letter slipped.

 

So the coupling erases the stored message well before it makes the metronomes swing together.

What this is. A simulation, not a recording. The model follows James Pantaleone, “Synchronization of metronomes,” Am. J. Phys. 70, 992 (2002). Each metronome is a pendulum, θ″ + ω² sin θ + μ[(θ/θv)² − 1] θ′ = −(x″/l) cos θ. The van der Pol term (the μ term) stands in for the escapement, the mechanism that gives a real metronome a small push each swing. It settles the swing near 0.75 rad (θv = 0.375 rad, μ = 0.4 s−1). The plank obeys x″/l = −β Σ d²(sin θj)/dt² − γx′/l − κx/l, with β = m/(M + Nm), roller friction γ = 0.3 s−1, and a weak centering spring κ = 0.2 s−2 that keeps the plank from drifting off the rollers. The spring's rate is far below the swing rate. The plank's acceleration depends on every arm's acceleration, so all accelerations are solved together each step. That is a rank-one linear system, solved with the Sherman–Morrison formula. Integration is fourth-order Runge–Kutta with a fixed step of 1 ms of simulated time.

Parameters. A 0.75 rad swing runs slower than the small-angle rate. So the base small-angle rate ω/2π = … Hz is chosen at load so that a free metronome, measured, swings at … Hz. It ticks twice per swing, which is … ticks a minute on the dial. Each metronome's rate is offset in proportion to its interval's beat rate in 12-tone equal temperament (12-EDO, standard piano tuning), using the same interval table as the main page. Offsets are centered on the mean, and the spread slider sets the full range from slowest to fastest. The looseness slider sets the mass ratio Nm/(M + Nm), from 0 (on the floor) to 0.16. It is capped at 0.16 because in scans of this model at 0.2 with eleven metronomes, the in-phase state sometimes did not take over within 150 s. On screen, the plank's motion is drawn four times larger than in the model so it is visible (two and a half times on a narrow screen). The rollers turn at the rate that rolling without slipping would give for the drawn motion. The sliding weights sit higher on slower metronomes, with the offset exaggerated. The arms swing at their true angles.

The message. Phase is atan2(−θ′/Ω, θ), where Ω is the measured swing rate. Every metronome has a ghost, an uncoupled copy that starts from the metronome's state at the moment you press Write. Writing advances each metronome along its own cycle by the letter's phase (letter index × 2π/32). The ghost is not advanced. A letter is read as the phase gap to its ghost, rounded to one of 32 levels. It is marked slipped the first time that rounded value differs from what was written. With the plank on the floor, metronome and ghost obey identical equations. The gap then wobbles only by the small unevenness of the phase estimate, which in tests stayed under 1.7 degrees, against a slip margin of 5.6 degrees. Times in the slip log and the chart are simulated seconds. Sound is a filtered noise click at each center crossing, scheduled to the simulated time.
