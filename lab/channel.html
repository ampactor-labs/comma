A Letter in a Piano

Every piano in every church basement between here and Moab is a little out of tune, and nobody calls the sheriff about it. That slack is room. You can hide a letter in it, thirty characters, written in cents, where only somebody with a strobe tuner and a bad attitude would ever think to look.

The tuning-channel note did the arithmetic on paper. This page goes ahead and does it. Type a letter and the page bends the piano's tuning away from the official curve, a cent here and two there. Then a humid summer gets its turn, then a man with a tuning hammer, and then you read back whatever is left.

01

Thirty characters, in cents

The official tuning is public. Equal temperament, every fifth shaved by the same sliver, plus the stretch: treble a little sharp, bass a little flat, because real strings are stiff and their overtones run sharp. Any tuner can recite it. The message is whatever you do that the official version didn't ask for.

There are three places to put it. Each of the twelve pitch classes can lean a few cents, the way the old well temperaments did, and nobody fainted. Each of the 88 keys can sit 2 cents sharp or flat of where the curve wants it. And on the 60 notes that carry three strings, the third string can sit a cent or so off its two partners. The ear takes that for warmth, if it notices at all.

02

The paved road and the shoulder

Every road the government builds comes with a map, and so does this one. The reference is the pavement: twelve equal semitones bent into a long lazy S by the stretch, flat down in the bass, sharp up in the treble. The letter is a line of boot prints a few feet off the shoulder, out where the tumbleweeds are. Close enough to the road that nobody pulls over.

The public reference

The cyan curve is the reference stretch this page assumes, a Railsback-like shape running from about −25 cents at the bottom A to +25 at the top C. It is drawn to look like the published curves, not measured from a piano. Dots are the keys as the piano stands right now, the same numbers as the needles above, at true scale.

The note guessed about 290 raw bits, at six levels per pitch class and three per key. Six and three are not powers of two, and I wasn't about to do arithmetic coding by headlamp, so this page rounds down to levels that pack: four, two and four. The table is built from the page's own layout.

03

A humid summer

Humidity swells the soundboard and the pitch climbs, but not everywhere at once and not evenly. It goes a region at a time, the way a thunderstorm walks across a mesa and leaves the next one dry. In coding terms that is burst noise, errors that come in bunches, and Reed–Solomon codes eat bunches. Each five-bit symbol lives in neighbouring strings, and neighbouring symbols go to different codewords, so a storm over one octave spends its damage on two codewords instead of one.

The reader isn't a fool either. It knows summers move whole regions, so before it commits to anything it guesses the drift, subtracts it and guesses again. After a read, the thin green line across the needles is that guess.

04

The coupling constant with a face

Weather is not the enemy. Weather is dumb, and dumb noise is exactly what codes are for. The enemy is the piano tuner, who is the coupling constant with a face: a decent, competent professional whose whole craft is getting everything to agree. He shows up with a tuning hammer and a felt mute and a thermos of bad coffee and he relaxes every deviation back to the consensus. The ones that were nearly right go first, because a string a hair off takes one tap.

The tuner here works the keyboard in sixteen passes, back and forth, and on each pass moves every string at most half a cent toward where the reference wants it. That is a bounded pull, the same shape as the coupling on the main page. Send him in and watch the counts in the readout.

I can't tell you whether you'll hear it. Nobody has measured it, which is the soft spot in the whole note. The listening test exists to measure exactly that.

The code. Reed–Solomon over GF(32) with primitive polynomial x5+x2+1, so one symbol is one character of the register's alphabet. Generator roots α0…α7; two shortened codewords of 23 symbols, 15 data and 8 parity each, RS(23,15), each correcting up to four wrong symbols. Decoding is syndromes, Berlekamp–Massey, Chien search and Forney. The decoder supports erasures, but the reader doesn't use them: in this model most wrong readings are confidently wrong, because whole regions drift together, and in offline tests marking doubtful symbols as erasures raised miscorrections without raising successes. A self-test runs when the page loads.

The layout. Temperament: each pitch class offset by −4.5, −1.5, +1.5 or +4.5 cents (2 bits, Gray-coded). Lean: each key ±2 cents from its pitch class and the stretch (1 bit). Unisons: on keys 29–88 (C♯3 to C8, taken as the trichords; makers differ) the third string sits −1.5, −0.5, +0.5 or +1.5 cents from its pair (2 bits). The bit stream runs temperament first, then key by key from the bass, cut into 5-bit symbols in that order; symbol i goes to codeword i mod 2. The letter's character i is symbol i, so the letter reads left to right up the keyboard and the parity sits in the treble. The last two bits, on the top C's third string, are spare.

The reader. It reads each key's pitch as the mean of its pair of strings and each third string against that pair. It decides each pitch class's temperament jointly over that class's seven or eight keys, then each key's lean, then each unison to the nearest level. Before deciding it tracks drift: four rounds of read, smooth the leftovers across the keyboard with a Gaussian five keys wide, subtract.

The weather and the tuner. A summer at severity s adds s × 6 cents times a seeded smooth random field (six Gaussian bumps, a swell in the middle of the compass and a tilt, scaled so its largest value is 1), plus independent jitter of s × 0.45 cents standard deviation on every string. Each press is a new summer. The tuner makes 16 passes, moving each string at most 0.5 cent toward its target per pass and landing within 0.2 cent once there. Neither model is fitted to a real piano. Because the tuner's pull is bounded, cells written close to the reference are bound to go first; the page shows the note's claim working under that assumption, and doesn't test it.

What is assumed and not measured. The two numbers the whole budget rests on are unmeasured: how far a tuning can wander before a listener notices (taken here as ±4.5 cents on temperament plus ±2 per key and ±1.5 on a unison), and how finely a strobe can read a string that has been drifting (taken as a 1-cent level spacing on unisons). Tighten or loosen them and the budget moves with them. The sound is synthetic, six harmonics per string with a little inharmonicity from Kit.sound.pluck, not a recorded piano, and the reference piano here has perfect unisons, which no real one does; a real piano's own spread would hide the letter better. The listening test is where the first of those thresholds gets measured.
