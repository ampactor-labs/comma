Back country · 08 · Plants and electrodes

A comodulogram for a sunflower

A sunflower has no neurons. Not one. It has cells that leak ions and a stem that carries slow electrical waves after a deer steps on it, and if you tape two electrodes to a leaf you get a voltage that wiggles at several speeds at once. Tape the same electrodes to a lump of agar and you get wiggles too. Electrode chemistry oscillates. That is the first thing to get straight out here.

Brain people have a test for whether a fast rhythm rides on a slow one: bin the slow wave's phase, see whether the fast wave swells at some part of it, draw the grid. They call the grid a comodulogram. The note this page grew out of says that nobody has pointed one at a plant, as far as its author could find. Here is the tool, run first on an oscillator bank whose insides we know, then handed over for whatever you have recorded.

01

The bank refuses to fake it

Before anybody hauls a Faraday cage out to a sunflower patch, try the test on something you understand. The piano's eleven tuning errors make a bank of oscillators (that's the main page). A few of them beat slowly, under a hertz. A few beat fast, between about five and nine. Sum the whole bank into one wire, the way an electrode sums a leaf, and ask whether the fast beats swell with the slow ones.

The main page's bank couples by addition: each oscillator gets nudged toward the average of all of them. Riding requires multiplication. Something slow has to work a fast thing's volume knob. So there is a second bank here, the same in every bolt except that the average's real part sets each oscillator's gain. One of these ought to light up. If the additive one lights up, the test is lying, and I would rather learn that here than in a greenhouse.

The note behind this page reported a flat grid at every coupling, largest z 2.9, from a script that never made it into the repo. So I ran it again in the open, and the box above is that rerun, done fresh in your browser every time. One more thing turned up along the way. The note's protocol calls for circular-shift surrogates: slide the fast envelope against the slow phase and see how often chance does as well. On a record this short, with a slow wave this regular, the slid copies are mostly copies of each other, so chance looks stingier than it is. Set the surrogates to circular shift and run 150 or 300 seconds: when I did, the additive bank lit a cell that block shuffling and a 600-second record both put out again.

02

Bring your own plant

Got a recording? A leaf, a dead stem, a dish of agar, your own forearm. One column of numbers, or time and value, comma or tab or whatever. Set the sample rate, set the bands, run it. Your browser reads the file and it goes nowhere else.

Calibrate your eye first on the three canned signals. The coupled one should burn. The noise should stay dark. The spiky one is the trap the note warns about: a slow wave with a sharp spike in it carries harmonics locked to itself, and those harmonics look exactly like a fast rhythm riding a slow one. It lights up with no coupling in it at all. Then spike removal takes the costume off.

03

What a lit cell is worth

Not much, and not nothing. A lit cell is a statistical dependence between two filtered views of one voltage. It says organization. It does not say anybody is home, and whoever tells you a sunflower is thinking because a grid turned orange is selling something, probably a subscription.

A dark grid proves nothing either. The additive bank mixes its inputs across time; the main page's readout measures that mixing climbing to 11.5% at K = 8, and this grid can't see any of it, because one wire carries only a linear sum of the oscillators and the mixing lives in their squares. Try the nonlinear views of a recording before you call the plant simple.

If you go out and record (from the note's protocol)

  • Surface electrodes on a leaf and the stem, a soil reference. Front end at 1012 Ω or better, DC-coupled or high-passed no higher than 0.001 Hz, 24-bit ADC at 250 Hz, all of it inside a Faraday enclosure, temperature and humidity logged.
  • Six hours per condition at least: undisturbed, a light step, a watering, one wound. Analyze event windows and quiet windows separately; never pool them.
  • Phase bands log-spaced over 0.003 to 0.3 Hz, amplitude bands over 2 to 25 Hz, 200 or more surrogates a cell, false-discovery correction across the grid.
  • The controls are the experiment: a dead or cut plant and an agar dummy under identical electrodes, a mains notch, and every lit cell re-run on a spike-removed copy.

The comma never closed. The bank remembers because it never agreed. Maybe the sunflower turns for the same reason, and maybe it just turns. Go find out. Bring water.

How the numbers are made, and where they can go wrong. Filters: the record's mean is removed, its ends are tapered over two cycles of the slowest phase band (at most 10% of the record a side) and zero-padded to a power of two; each band is cut from one FFT with raised-cosine skirts 20% of the band's width, doubled on the positive side to give the analytic signal, and the tapered ends are trimmed before anything is counted. Narrow phase bands ring for longer than that trim covers, so do not trust cells built on bands narrower than a few cycles of the record's length. An amplitude band must be wider than twice the phase frequency it is tested against or the sidebands that carry the coupling fall outside it; for loaded records the page widens amplitude bands to at least twice the top phase edge and tells you when it did. Statistic: Tort's modulation index on 18 phase bins. Each cell is compared with surrogates: circular shift moves the amplitude envelope against the phase by a random offset of at least one second or one cycle of the slowest phase band, whichever is longer; block shuffle cuts the envelope into blocks of that length and reorders them. The p-value is (1 + surrogates at least as large) / (1 + surrogates), so the smallest possible p is 1/(n+1), and with m cells Benjamini–Hochberg at q = 0.05 needs at least about 20m surrogates before any cell can pass. The z-score is against the surrogates' mean and spread and is shown for comparison with the note; the outlines come from p, not from z, because the surrogate distribution of the index is skewed and a normal tail read off z overstates significance. Calibration, measured in Node on this page's own analysis code with white noise, 150 s at 50 Hz, the bank's 12-cell grid, 1000 surrogates, 150 records each: circular shift lit at least one cell in 13% of records, block shuffle in 8%, against a nominal 5%. A slow wave that keeps its phase for many cycles leaves only a handful of truly different surrogates in a short record, and the test gets bolder than it should. Long records fix this and nothing else does. The note's own warning stands: 140 seconds gives about fifty cycles of the slowest bank band, which is marginal, and a plant phase band at 0.01 Hz needs hours of stable recording. The bank: the sum of the real parts of the eleven oscillators at 50 samples a second, driven by the main page's seeded random input, first 10 seconds discarded, the same K for both banks; the multiplicative mode is kit.js's, where the mean field's real part scales each oscillator. Spike removal subtracts a running median, flags samples beyond k times the median absolute deviation, then either subtracts the average spike (fitted in size to each one) or clips at the threshold; template subtraction assumes the spikes look alike, clipping leaves flat-topped bumps that still carry harmonics, and either can add structure of its own, so look at the trace. Loaded files are read in the browser and never sent anywhere; the limit is 221 samples.
